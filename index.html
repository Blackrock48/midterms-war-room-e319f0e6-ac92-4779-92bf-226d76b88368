Chamber 1 · the fight that is open

Senate 100 seats · 6-year terms · 35 up this year · 51 = majority

The upper house, like a stronger version of the Belgian Senate: two senators per state regardless of population. It confirms judges and ministers and ratifies treaties. Republicans hold it 53–47 now. Only a third of seats is contested each cycle, and this year's third includes the 13 races tracked below. Democrats need a net gain of 4, because a 50–50 tie is broken by Vice-President Vance.

Chamber 2 · widely expected to flip

House of Representatives 435 seats · 2-year terms · all up · 218 = majority

The lower house, seats by population, like the Kamer. It controls spending and can impeach. Republicans started this term with a thin 220–215 majority (a few seats are vacant at any time). All 435 districts vote on 3 November; the forecasts give Democrats roughly 90% to take it, so the suspense there is the size of the swing, not the direction. House detail is in its own section further down.

          Reading the spread

          • Every model and market agrees on the direction: Democrats take the House and are favoured for the Senate. They disagree on how sure to be about the Senate — from DDHQ's 65% to Poliwave's 85%.
          • The gap is about correlation. If polls are off by 2–3 points, they are usually off in the same direction everywhere. A Republican miss of that size turns Iowa, Kansas, Alaska and Ohio at once and keeps the Senate red; a Democratic miss produces a 54–55 seat wave.
          • Historical anchors: 2018 (Trump at ~−10) gave Democrats +40 House seats; 2006 (Bush at ~−20) gave them +31 House and +6 Senate. Trump enters this night at roughly −19 to −23. The final ratings (D+24 House, D+6 Senate) sit squarely in that 2006 pattern.
          • What would count as a surprise: Republicans holding the Senate (≈15–35%), Democrats winning fewer than 225 House seats, or Democrats taking Iowa and South Carolina and Nebraska (the long tail of a 55-seat night).
